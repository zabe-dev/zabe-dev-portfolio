Today I turn 32.

I don’t have everything figured out, and I’m starting to think nobody really does. Most of my life has been a mix of learning as I go, making mistakes, solving whatever problem is in front of me, and trying to become a little better at handling the next one.

I’ve been supporting myself since high school. I found coding through online games, started setting up forums for gaming communities, and slowly turned those early skills into remote work. My path has never looked very traditional, but it has taught me a lot.

Here are 32 things I’ve learned in 32 years.

## 1. Starting badly is better than waiting perfectly

Most things I’m proud of began before I felt ready. You learn more from an imperfect first attempt than from another month of thinking about it.

## 2. Being able to figure things out is a real skill

You won’t always know the answer. Knowing how to search, test, read the documentation, and stay with a problem matters more.

## 3. Curiosity can change your life

I started installing forums because I wanted to be part of gaming communities. I didn’t know that curiosity would eventually lead me into development, hosting, automation, and security.

## 4. Small opportunities count

Not every opportunity looks important when it arrives. A small task, a forum setup, or a website fix can teach you something that becomes useful years later.

## 5. You don’t need a traditional path to keep moving

There are many ways to learn and build a life. Mine came through remote work, real problems, online communities, and a lot of trial and error.

## 6. Documentation is part of the work

Reading the docs isn’t a last resort. It is usually where the answer has been waiting the whole time.

## 7. Ask better questions

A clear question saves time. Before looking for a solution, understand what is actually broken and what a successful result should look like.

## 8. Fix the simple thing first

The problem is often smaller than it looks. Check the obvious causes before rebuilding everything.

## 9. Keep backups

You only need to lose important work once to understand this lesson.

## 10. Rest is not wasted time

Some problems become easier after stepping away. A tired brain can turn a small bug into a three-hour mystery.

## 11. Games can teach useful things

Games taught me patience, teamwork, systems, strategy, and how much I enjoy solving problems. They also introduced me to the communities where I first learned web development.

## 12. The internet can be a classroom

You can learn almost anything if you are willing to read, experiment, and stay curious. The difficult part is knowing what deserves your attention.

## 13. Not everything needs to become work

It is okay to enjoy something without trying to monetize it, optimize it, or turn it into a project.

## 14. Simple usually lasts longer

This applies to websites, tools, routines, and explanations. Complexity should earn its place.

## 15. Make things easier for the next person

Clear names, useful notes, and maintainable code are small forms of respect for whoever comes after you—including your future self.

## 16. Security starts with paying attention

Many issues appear because somebody assumed a system would behave correctly. Look closely, question assumptions, and test responsibly.

## 17. Responsible disclosure matters

Finding a vulnerability is only part of the job. Reporting it clearly, protecting affected people, and respecting confidentiality matter just as much.

## 18. You can be proud without knowing everything

There will always be someone who knows more. That doesn’t erase how far you have come.

## 19. You can change your mind

Changing direction after learning something new is not failure. Stubbornly staying wrong is worse.

## 20. Consistency is quieter than motivation

Motivation comes and goes. Small repeated effort is less exciting, but it is what usually finishes the work.

## 21. Your work doesn’t need to impress everyone

It should be useful to the people it is for. Trying to satisfy everybody usually makes the result less clear.

## 22. Learn the basics deeply

Tools change quickly. Fundamentals make it easier to understand whatever replaces them.

## 23. Automate the boring parts carefully

Automation is useful when you understand the task first. Automating confusion only makes mistakes happen faster.

## 24. Say when you don’t know

Pretending wastes time and creates bigger problems. “I don’t know yet, but I’ll look into it” is a useful answer.

## 25. Being dependable matters

Skill gets attention, but people remember whether you communicated, followed through, and showed up when something went wrong.

## 26. Protect your time

Not every message is urgent. Not every opportunity is right. Time spent on one thing is time you cannot spend somewhere else.

## 27. Compare less

The internet makes it easy to compare your unfinished work with somebody else’s best result. That comparison rarely helps.

## 28. Keep evidence of your progress

Save old projects, notes, screenshots, and small wins. Memory is bad at showing you how much you have improved.

## 29. A good life can look ordinary

Peace, useful work, enough rest, and time for things you enjoy are not small achievements.

## 30. People rarely have the full story

Be slower to judge. Everyone is carrying context you cannot see.

## 31. It is never too late to make the thing

I put off making my own portfolio for a long time. It exists now. That matters more than how long it took.

## 32. Keep something to look forward to

A project, a game, a new skill, or a quiet day—something small is enough. Life feels lighter when there is something ahead you genuinely want to reach.

Thirty-two doesn’t feel like having all the answers. It feels more like being comfortable with continuing to learn them.
