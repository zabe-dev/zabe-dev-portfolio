I’ve built and managed websites for other people, but making one for myself kept getting pushed aside. There was always another client task, a bug to look into, or something new I wanted to learn first. A portfolio sounded simple until I had to decide what it should actually say about me.

I knew I didn’t want a loud site with huge text, too many sections, or animations everywhere. I wanted something quiet and easy to read: mostly black and white, a little green, and enough space for the things that matter. Getting there took more small changes than I expected. I moved the navigation around, rewrote the introduction several times, changed the fonts, adjusted the dark mode, and removed anything that felt too formal or unnecessary.

The hardest part wasn’t the code. It was describing what I do without making it sound like a résumé or a sales pitch. My path into development has never been very traditional. I started by setting up forums and fixing websites for online gaming communities, then kept learning through remote work and whatever problems came next. I wanted the site to sound like that story instead of a list of job titles.

## What I ended up building

The site uses Next.js, React, and TypeScript. The design stays intentionally simple, with Lora for headings, DM Sans for most text, and DM Mono for smaller labels and social links. It has light and dark themes, project and education sections, a short version of my story, and now this blog.

The blog posts live in Markdown files. That was important to me because I wanted adding a post to feel simple. I can create a file, add a title, date, excerpt, and tags, then focus on the actual note instead of managing a dashboard.

I also spent time on the less visible parts: breaking the page into reusable components, fixing the production build, cleaning up unused files, making the mobile layout easier to use, and making sure the site stays out of search results for now.

## What I learned

Working on your own site is strangely harder than working from a client brief. There is no clear finish line, and every choice feels personal. It’s easy to keep changing things because there is always another font, layout, or sentence to try.

At some point, I had to stop treating the site like a perfect final version. It just needs to be an honest version of where I am now. I can keep adding projects, updating what I’m learning, and sharing useful notes as I go.

For now, I’m happy that it finally exists.
